## 1. Boat & Yacht Classifications

### Size Classifications (US Federal / USCG)

| Class   | Length                | Examples                                          |
|---------|-----------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| Class A | Less than 16 ft       | Dinghies, jon boats, small inflatables, kayaks     |
| Class 1 | 16 ft to < 26 ft      | Runabouts, ski boats, small pontoons, PWCs         |
| Class 2 | 26 ft to < 40 ft      | Medium cruisers, larger fishing boats, small yachts|
| Class 3 | 40 ft to < 65 ft      | Large yachts, sportfishing vessels, small commercial|
| Class 4 | 65 ft and over        | Megayachts, large commercial vessels               |

### Yacht Size Categories

| Category      | Length (ft)  | Length (m)   | Typical Crew | Key Features                                                   |
|---------------|-------------|-------------|-------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| Small Yacht   | 33--80      | 10--24      | 0--3        | Good balance of comfort and manageability; fewer amenities      |
| Medium Yacht  | 80--164     | 24--50      | 4--12       | Multiple guest cabins, advanced entertainment, longer trips     |

### Popular Rental Boat Types

#### Pontoon Boats
- **Hull**: Flat deck on 2--3 aluminum tubes (pontoons); tritoons have 3 tubes for better performance
- **Length**: 16--30 ft typical
- **Capacity**: High -- a 17 ft pontoon seats 8--9; a 28 ft seats 15--18
- **Best for**: Calm inland waters, entertaining, family cruising, fishing
- **Speed**: Typically 18--25 mph; performance tritoons up to 40+ mph
- **Pros**: Extremely stable, spacious, easy to operate, high passenger capacity
- **Cons**: Not suitable for rough/open water, limited storage below deck

#### Bowriders
- **Hull**: V-shaped fiberglass with open bow seating
- **Length**: 17--30 ft typical
- **Capacity**: 6--12 passengers
- **Best for**: Watersports (tubing, skiing, wakeboarding), day cruising, families
- **Speed**: 30--50+ mph
- **Pros**: Versatile, sporty, good for water activities
- **Cons**: Less stable than pontoons, can be wet in chop

#### Center Console Boats
- **Hull**: Deep-V fiberglass hull, steering console centered
- **Length**: 17--42 ft typical
- **Capacity**: 4--12 passengers
- **Best for**: Sport fishing (inshore and offshore), watersports, rough water
- **Speed**: 30--60+ mph
- **Pros**: 360-degree fishability, handles rough water, self-bailing cockpit
- **Cons**: Limited shade/shelter, fewer comfort amenities

#### Cabin Cruisers
- **Hull**: V-hull fiberglass with enclosed cabin
- **Length**: 25--45 ft typical
- **Capacity**: 4--12 passengers, 2--6 sleeping berths
- **Best for**: Overnight stays, weekend trips, extended cruising
- **Features**: Staterooms, galley (kitchen), head (bathroom), helm with hardtop
- **Pros**: Can sleep aboard, weather protection, kitchen/bathroom onboard
- **Cons**: Higher cost, more complex to operate, requires larger slip

#### Deck Boats
- **Hull**: Modified V-hull with wider, flared bow
- **Length**: 18--28 ft typical
- **Capacity**: 8--14 passengers
- **Best for**: Entertaining, day trips, watersports, fishing
- **Pros**: More deck space than bowriders, versatile layout
- **Cons**: Not as fast or agile as bowriders

#### Sailboats
- **Hull**: Mono-hull or catamaran with mast and sails
- **Length**: 20--60+ ft
- **Capacity**: 4--12 (day sailing); 2--8 (overnight)
- **Best for**: Sailing enthusiasts, sunset cruises, multi-day voyages
- **Pros**: Fuel efficient, quiet, traditional experience
- **Cons**: Dependent on wind, requires sailing knowledge, slower

#### Catamarans
- **Hull**: Twin hulls connected by a platform deck
- **Length**: 30--60+ ft
- **Capacity**: 8--20+ passengers
- **Best for**: Group charters, multi-day trips, stability in open water
- **Pros**: Extremely stable (hard to capsize), spacious living areas, shallow draft
- **Cons**: More expensive, wider beam requires wider slips

#### Personal Watercraft (PWC/Jet Ski)
- **Length**: 8--13 ft
- **Capacity**: 1--3 riders
- **Best for**: Thrill-seekers, short rides, watersport tow vehicles
- **Speed**: 40--70 mph
- **Rental rate**: Typically $75--$150/hour

#### Houseboats
- **Length**: 30--75 ft
- **Capacity**: 4--16 sleeping berths
- **Best for**: Vacation rentals on lakes and rivers, multi-day stays
- **Features**: Full kitchen, multiple bedrooms, bathrooms, living areas
- **Rental rate**: $300--$1,500+ per day

## 2. Boat Rental Industry Overview

### Market Size & Growth
- Global boat rental market valued at approximately $18.41 billion (2025)
- Projected to reach $24.75 billion by 2030
- Boatsetter + Getmyboat merger created combined entity with $500M+ in bookings across 50 countries

### Key Market Drivers
- Growth of the "experience economy" over ownership
- Rise of peer-to-peer rental platforms (Airbnb model for boats)
- Increasing interest in water-based recreation post-pandemic
- Younger demographics preferring rental access over boat ownership

- Over 12 million registered recreational boats in the US
- Florida alone has over 1 million registered boats
- Florida's recreational boating industry contributes $30+ billion annually to the state economy

### Pricing Factors
- **Season**: July is peak for pontoons; August peak for yachts; December--January cheapest
- **Location**: Miami, Fort Lauderdale, Tampa command premium pricing
- **Day of week**: Weekdays are 20--40% cheaper than weekends
- **Duration**: Longer bookings often get per-hour discounts (half-day, full-day, multi-day)
- **Fuel**: May or may not be included; always clarify with the listing
- **Captain**: Adds $150--$500+/day depending on vessel size
- **Extras**: Watersports equipment, catering, decorations are typically add-ons

### Common Minimum Booking Durations
- Jet Ski: 30 minutes to 1 hour
- Small boats: 2 hours
- Pontoons: 2--4 hours
- Yachts: 4 hours (half-day)
- Luxury yachts: Full day or multi-day

## 4. Insurance & Liability

### Key Coverage Types for Charter/Rental Operations

| Coverage Type              | What It Covers                                           | Typical Limits                  |
|---------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Hull Insurance            | Physical damage to the vessel                            | Based on vessel value            |
| P&I (Protection & Indemnity) | Liability for injury, death, property damage          | $300K -- $5M+                    |
| Medical Payments          | Medical bills for anyone injured on rental boats         | Starting at ~$25,000/person      |
| Environmental Liability   | Cleanup costs from oil/fuel leaks                        | Required by many marinas         |
| Uninsured Boater          | Covers damage from uninsured third parties               | Varies                           |
| Guest Passenger            | Covers injuries to paying passengers                     | Required for charter operations  |

### Key Insurance Facts
- Personal boat insurance does NOT cover rentals or charter use
- Commercial boat insurance required for any rental/charter operation
- Bundling hull + P&I + general liability saves 15--24% vs. separate policies
- Fleet policies available for multi-vessel operations at reduced rates
- Florida requires renters to show proof of insurance (Boating Safety Act of 2022)
- Many marinas require environmental liability coverage before allowing dock access
- Peer-to-peer platforms (Boatsetter, Getmyboat) typically include insurance in their booking

### Renter Considerations
- Most platforms include basic liability coverage in the rental price
- Security deposits range from $250 to $5,000+ depending on vessel value
- Damage waivers are often available for an additional fee ($20--$100/day)
- Renters should verify what their personal homeowner's or umbrella policy covers

## 5. Nautical Terminology Glossary

### Direction & Position

| Term          | Definition                                                      |
|---------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Bow**       | The forward (front) part of a boat                              |
| **Stern**     | The back (rear) end of a boat                                   |
| **Port**      | The left side when facing the bow (red navigation light)        |
| **Starboard** | The right side when facing the bow (green navigation light)     |
| **Aft**       | Toward the stern                                                |
| **Fore / Forward** | Toward the bow                                             |
| **Amidships** | The center of the boat (length-wise)                            |
| **Beam**      | The widest point of a boat; also used for "off the beam" (perpendicular) |
| **Abeam**     | At right angles to the centerline of the boat                   |
| **Windward**  | The side from which the wind is blowing                         |
| **Leeward (lee)** | The side sheltered from the wind                            |
| **Astern**    | Behind the boat                                                 |
| **Ahead**     | In front of the boat                                            |

### Boat Structure

| Term           | Definition                                                     |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Hull**       | The body/shell of the boat                                      |
| **Keel**       | The central structural base running bow to stern (bottom)       |
| **Deck**       | The flat surface forming the top of the hull                    |
| **Gunwale**    | The upper edge of a boat's side (pronounced "gunnel")           |
| **Transom**    | The flat surface at the stern                                   |
| **Helm**       | The steering station/wheel                                      |
| **Galley**     | The kitchen on a boat                                           |
| **Head**       | The bathroom/toilet on a boat                                   |
| **Berth**      | A sleeping space; also a dock space                             |
| **Stateroom**  | A private cabin/bedroom                                         |
| **Cockpit**    | The open area where the boat is controlled                      |
| **Flybridge**  | An upper open-air helm station on a yacht                       |
| **Swim platform** | A flat area at the stern for boarding from water             |
| **Cleat**      | A metal fitting for securing ropes/lines                        |
| **Anchor rode** | The line and/or chain connecting the anchor to the boat        |

### Measurements & Performance

| Term           | Definition                                                     |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Draft**      | Depth of the boat below the waterline to the lowest point       |
| **Freeboard**  | Height of the hull above the waterline                          |
| **LOA**        | Length Overall -- total length of the boat                       |
| **Beam**       | Maximum width of the boat                                       |
| **Displacement** | Weight of water displaced by the hull (effectively the boat's weight) |
| **Knot**       | 1 nautical mile per hour (1.15 mph / 1.85 km/h)               |
| **Nautical mile** | 1.15 statute miles / 1.85 km                                |
| **Wake**       | Waves produced by a moving boat                                 |
| **No-wake zone** | Area where boats must travel at idle speed (minimal wake)     |

### Operations & Maneuvering

| Term           | Definition                                                     |
|----------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Underway**   | A vessel that is not anchored, moored, or aground               |
| **Making way** | A vessel moving through the water under its own power           |
| **Casting off** | Releasing the dock lines to depart                             |
| **Mooring**    | Securing a boat to a fixed point (buoy, dock, anchor)           |
| **Docking**    | Bringing a boat alongside a dock                                |
| **Trimming**   | Adjusting the angle of the drive/outboard for optimal performance|
| **Planing**    | When a boat reaches speed sufficient to ride on top of the water|
| **Heeling**    | Leaning to one side (common in sailboats)                       |
| **Tacking**    | Zigzagging into the wind (sailing)                              |
| **Jibing**     | Turning the stern through the wind (sailing)                    |
| **Man overboard (MOB)** | Emergency: a person has fallen into the water            |

### Common Phrases

| Phrase                  | Meaning                                                 |
|------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------|
| "All hands on deck"    | Everyone is needed                                       |
| "Batten down the hatches" | Prepare for bad weather                               |
| "Give way"             | Yield right of way                                       |
| "Stand on"             | Maintain your course and speed (you have right of way)   |
| "Mayday"               | International distress call (imminent danger to life)    |

## 7. Boat Party Planning

### Capacity Guidelines

| Boat Type        | Typical Max Passengers | Comfortable Party Size | Notes                              |
|-----------------|----------------------|----------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Pontoon (22 ft) | 10--12               | 8--10                | Best value for small groups         |
| Pontoon (28 ft) | 15--18               | 12--15               | Ideal for medium parties            |
| Deck boat       | 10--14               | 8--12                |                                     |
| Cabin cruiser   | 8--12                | 6--10                | Has indoor/outdoor space            |
| Yacht (50 ft)   | 12--20               | 10--16               | Premium experience                  |
| Yacht (80 ft)   | 30--50               | 20--40               | Corporate events, weddings          |
| Yacht (100+ ft) | 50--100+             | 40--80               | Large events with crew              |

**Important**: The boat's capacity plate (set by the manufacturer and USCG) is the legal maximum. Never exceed it. Plan for comfort at 70--80% of max capacity.

### Food & Catering

**Best Practices**:
- Keep the menu simple and low-mess (finger foods, wraps, pre-portioned items)
- Account for motion sickness: avoid heavy, greasy foods
- Provide plenty of water and non-alcoholic options
- Plan for 1.5x normal food portions (fresh air increases appetite)

**No glass allowed**: Never bring glassware on a boat. Broken glass is dangerous to guests, damaging to boat equipment, and harmful to marine life. Use plastic, aluminum, or acrylic drinkware exclusively.

**Popular Boat Party Menus**:
- Appetizer trays: cheese boards, fruit, shrimp cocktail, crudites
- Main course: wraps, sliders, tacos, grilled items (if boat has a grill)
- Sides: chips, pasta salad, coleslaw
- Desserts: cookies, brownies, pre-cut cake (avoid frosted items in heat)

**Catering Costs**: Professional catering for boat parties typically runs $25--$75/person depending on menu complexity and location.

### Entertainment

- Create a curated playlist (waterproof Bluetooth speakers recommended)
- Consider hiring a DJ for larger yachts
- Water toys: inflatable slides, floating mats, paddleboards, snorkel gear
- Games: water balloon fights, fishing rods, trivia
- Photo opportunities: sunrise/sunset timing, scenic backdrops
- Theme parties: tropical luau, white party, decades theme, nautical theme

### Party Types & Considerations

| Event Type           | Ideal Boat Size | Duration   | Special Needs                        |
|---------------------|----------------|-----------|---------------------------------------|
| Birthday party      | 30--60 ft      | 3--4 hrs  | Decorations, cake, music              |
| Bachelor/ette       | 40--80 ft      | 4--6 hrs  | Sound system, dance floor, bar        |
| Corporate event     | 60--120 ft     | 3--5 hrs  | A/V equipment, catering, branding     |
| Wedding             | 80--150+ ft    | 5--8 hrs  | Officiant, photographer, full catering|
| Sunset cruise       | 25--50 ft      | 2--3 hrs  | West-facing route, appetizers, wine   |
| Family gathering    | 24--40 ft      | 3--5 hrs  | Kid-friendly activities, shade        |
| Holiday celebration | 40--80 ft      | 3--5 hrs  | Themed decorations, fireworks viewing |

### Safety for Boat Parties

1. **Designate a sober skipper** or hire a professional captain
2. **Brief all guests** on safety rules, life jacket locations, and emergency procedures
3. **Assign a boat monitor**: a sober, responsible person familiar with boating safety
4. **Headcount system**: count passengers at regular intervals
5. **Keep walkways clear**: secure all loose items, decorations, and equipment
6. **Children**: must wear life jackets at all times; designate a child-watching adult
7. **Sun protection**: provide sunscreen, shade options, and encourage hydration
8. **First aid kit**: always accessible and stocked
9. **Communication**: ensure working cell phone or VHF radio on board

## 8. Maritime Safety & Coast Guard Regulations

### Required Safety Equipment by Vessel Size

#### Class A: Under 16 ft

| Equipment               | Requirement                                              |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Life jackets (PFDs)    | 1 USCG-approved Type I, II, III, or V per person         |
| Fire extinguisher      | 1 B-1 type (if enclosed compartments/permanent fuel tanks)|
| Visual distress signals| Night signals required in coastal waters                  |
| Sound-producing device | Whistle, horn, or other means                            |
| Navigation lights      | Required between sunset and sunrise                       |

#### Class 1: 16 ft to < 26 ft

| Equipment               | Requirement                                              |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Life jackets           | 1 per person PLUS 1 Type IV throwable device              |
| Fire extinguisher      | 1 B-1 type minimum                                       |
| Visual distress signals| 3 day + 3 night signals (coastal waters)                  |
| Sound-producing device | Horn or whistle                                           |
| Navigation lights      | Required sunset to sunrise                                |
| Ventilation            | Required for enclosed fuel spaces                         |

#### Class 2: 26 ft to < 40 ft

| Equipment               | Requirement                                              |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Life jackets           | 1 per person + 1 Type IV throwable                        |
| Fire extinguisher      | 2 B-1 type OR 1 B-2 type                                 |
| Visual distress signals| 3 day + 3 night signals                                  |
| Sound-producing device | Horn or whistle audible for 0.5 nautical mile             |
| Navigation lights      | Required sunset to sunrise                                |
| Ventilation            | Required for enclosed fuel spaces                         |

#### Class 3: 40 ft to < 65 ft

| Equipment               | Requirement                                              |
|------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Life jackets           | 1 per person + 1 Type IV throwable                        |
| Fire extinguisher      | 3 B-1 type OR 1 B-1 + 1 B-2 type                        |
| Visual distress signals| 3 day + 3 night signals                                  |
| Sound-producing device | Horn audible for 1 nautical mile + bell                   |
| Navigation lights      | Required sunset to sunrise                                |

### Captain Licensing (USCG)

#### OUPV / Six-Pack License
- **Allows**: Up to 6 paying passengers + crew on uninspected vessels
- **Vessel size**: Up to 100 gross tons (typically 75--90 ft)
- **Requirements**:
  - Minimum 360 days of boating experience (90 in last 7 years)
  - A "sea day" = 4+ hours on the water within a 24-hour period
  - Must be at least 18 years old
  - US citizen or permanent resident
  - Pass physical exam and drug test
  - Valid CPR and First Aid certification
  - TWIC card (Transportation Worker Identification Credential)
  - Pass written examination
- **Renewal**: Every 5 years (no written exam; physical + drug test required)

#### Master License (50/100 Ton)
- **Allows**: More than 6 paying passengers on inspected vessels
- **Vessel size**: Up to 50 or 100 gross tons
- **Requirements**: More sea time, additional exams, higher standards
- **Needed for**: Party boats, dinner cruises, large charter operations

### Certificate of Inspection (COI)
- Required for vessels carrying more than 6 passengers for hire
- Issued by USCG after thorough vessel inspection
- Specifies maximum passenger capacity, crew requirements, allowed routes
- May restrict vessel to inland waterways, daytime only, or distance from shore
- Must be displayed prominently on board
- Annual inspections required

### Vessel Registration
- All motorized vessels must be registered in their state
- Vessels documented with USCG need a Certificate of Documentation
- Registration numbers must be displayed on the hull
- Commercial vessels (charter/rental) may need additional permits

### Important Federal Regulations
- **Rendering assistance**: Federal law requires boaters to assist anyone in danger at sea (when safe to do so)
- **Reporting accidents**: Must report to USCG/state if death, disappearance, injury requiring medical treatment, or property damage exceeding $2,000
- **Navigation rules**: International and Inland Rules of the Road (COLREGS)
- **Speed**: No federal speed limit on open water, but no-wake zones are enforced
- **Waste discharge**: It is illegal to discharge raw sewage within 3 nautical miles of shore

## 9. Weather Considerations

### Beaufort Wind Scale (Boating Reference)

| Force | Wind Speed (knots) | Wind Speed (mph) | Description       | Sea Conditions                     | Boating Guidance                       |
|-------|-------------------|------------------|--------------------|-------------------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| 0     | 0--1              | 0--1             | Calm               | Mirror-like sea                     | Perfect for all boats                  |
| 1     | 1--3              | 1--3             | Light air           | Ripples, no crests                  | Perfect for all boats                  |
| 2     | 4--6              | 4--7             | Light breeze        | Small wavelets                      | Ideal for sailing and all boats        |

### Practical Weather Guidelines for Renters

**Safe conditions for most rental boats**:
- Wind: Under 15 knots (17 mph)
- Waves: Under 2 feet
- Visibility: Greater than 1 mile
- No thunderstorms in forecast
- No small craft advisories

**Marginal conditions (experienced boaters only)**:
- Wind: 15--20 knots
- Waves: 2--4 feet
- Partly cloudy with isolated shower chance

**Unsafe conditions (cancel/postpone)**:
- Wind: Over 20 knots
- Waves: Over 4 feet
- Thunderstorms likely
- Small craft advisory or higher
- Dense fog (visibility under 0.5 miles)

**Boats under 25 ft should head to shore when winds approach 10 mph in open water.**

### Weather Planning Tips
1. **Check forecast morning of**: Use NOAA/NWS marine forecast for your specific area
2. **Watch for afternoon thunderstorms**: Common in Florida and Gulf Coast (May--September)
3. **Know the signs**: Darkening skies, sudden wind shifts, dropping barometric pressure
4. **Lightning**: If you can hear thunder, you are within strike range. Head to shore immediately.
5. **Fog**: Common in spring mornings on lakes and coasts; usually burns off by mid-morning
6. **Temperature**: Water temperature affects hypothermia risk; cold water is dangerous even in warm air
7. **Tides/currents**: Check tide tables; strong currents affect fuel consumption and navigation

## 10. Yacht Club Etiquette & Culture

### Dress Codes

#### Common Dress Categories

| Setting         | Men                                                  | Women                                              |
|----------------|------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------|

| **Semi-Formal**| Blazer, dress shirt, tie optional, slacks              | Cocktail dress, dress pants and blouse              |
| **Formal**     | Suit or tuxedo                                         | Evening gown, formal dress                          |

#### Universal No-Nos
- Bare feet inside clubhouse (boat shoes or dress shoes required)
- Bare torsos or shirtless anywhere on club premises
- Men's sleeveless shirts or tank tops
- Bathing suits without opaque cover-ups
- Flip-flops in dining areas
- Baseball caps indoors (most clubs); remove hats inside
- Denim cutoffs or ripped jeans

### Guest Etiquette
- Members are responsible for their guests' behavior and attire
- Guests should always be accompanied by their host member
- Sign the guest register upon arrival
- Do not use the club's facilities without your host present
- Thank the staff and your host

### Marina & Dock Etiquette
- **Ask permission** before stepping aboard someone else's vessel
- **Walk, don't run** on docks
- **Keep noise down** especially early morning and after 10 PM
- **Clean up after yourself**: no trash in the water or on docks
- **Respect dock lines**: never step on or move another boat's lines
- **Slow down in the marina**: no-wake zone always applies
- **Fly proper flags**: Yacht club burgee (flag) at the starboard spreader
- **Power cord etiquette**: neatly coil shore power cables
- **Offer help**: If you see someone docking alone, offer to catch a line

### Flag Etiquette
- US national ensign (flag) is flown from the stern or gaff
- Yacht club burgee at the bow staff or starboard spreader
- Flags are raised at 0800 and lowered at sunset
- A boat underway may fly the ensign continuously
- Never fly a tattered or faded flag

### Communication
- Use proper VHF radio etiquette (no profanity, keep transmissions brief)
- Hail a vessel on Channel 16 (distress and calling), then switch to a working channel
- Respond to radio calls with your vessel name
- Phone calls, videos, or audible device noises are not permitted inside club buildings

## 12. Alcohol & Legal Considerations

### Boating Under the Influence (BUI)

| Fact                                | Detail                                                    |
|------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------|
| Federal BAC limit                  | 0.08% (same as driving)                                   |
| Under 21 BAC limit                | 0.02% in most states                                     |
| Enforcement                       | USCG and state marine patrol can stop and test any boater |
| Fatality involvement               | ~50% of all boating fatalities involve alcohol (USCG data)|
| Penalties                          | Fines, imprisonment, license suspension, mandatory classes |
| Enhanced impairment                | Sun, wind, waves, and vibration amplify alcohol's effects  |

### Open Container Laws (Varies by State)

| State         | Open Containers on Boats? | Notes                                  |
|--------------|--------------------------|----------------------------------------|
| Florida      | Legal                    | Operator must stay under 0.08% BAC     |
| Texas        | Legal                    | No open container restriction on boats  |
| Wisconsin    | Legal                    | Operator may not drink while driving    |
| Washington   | Legal for passengers     | Operator may not drink while operating  |
| California   | Legal                    | Operator under 0.08% BAC               |
| Michigan     | Legal                    | Operator under 0.08% BAC               |
| Many states  | Legal for passengers     | Always illegal for operator to be impaired |

**Key principle**: In most US states, open containers of alcohol are legal on boats (unlike in cars), but the operator must remain below the legal BAC limit. BUI laws are enforced in all 50 states and by the USCG on federal waters.

### Recommendations for Charter/Rental Operations
- Always designate a sober operator or hire a captain
- Brief passengers on BUI laws during safety orientation
- Consider offering a beverage package as an upsell (controlled, responsible service)
- Keep records of safety briefings for liability protection

## Quick Reference: Frequently Asked Questions

### "Do I need a boating license to rent a boat?"
It depends on the state and charter type. For **bareboat** (self-drive) rentals, many states require a boating safety certificate or license. For **captained** charters, no license is needed -- the captain handles everything. Some states (like Florida) require a Boating Safety Education ID Card for operators born after a certain date.

### "What should I bring on a boat rental?"
- Sunscreen (reef-safe preferred)
- Sunglasses with strap
- Hat
- Non-marking shoes (boat shoes or rubber-soled sneakers)
- Light layers / windbreaker
- Towels
- Cooler with drinks and snacks (no glass!)
- Waterproof phone case
- Motion sickness medication (if prone)
- Valid ID

### "What's the difference between a yacht and a boat?"
Generally, a vessel is considered a yacht when it exceeds 33--40 feet in length. Yachts typically have living quarters (cabin, galley, head), higher-end finishes, and are designed for leisure cruising rather than fishing or utility.

### "How far in advance should I book?"
- **Peak season (summer, holidays)**: 2--4 weeks minimum; 1--2 months for luxury yachts
- **Shoulder season**: 1--2 weeks
- **Off-season**: Same-week or even same-day availability common
- **Special events (4th of July, Labor Day)**: Book 1--3 months ahead

### "Is it safe to boat with kids?"
Yes, with proper precautions:
- Children under 6 (or non-swimmers) must wear life jackets at all times
- Choose calm, protected waters (bays, lakes, rivers)
- Pontoons and catamarans offer the most stability
- Bring shade, sunscreen, snacks, and entertainment
- Brief children on safety rules before departure
- Hire a captain so parents can focus on the kids

### "What happens if the weather is bad?"
Most reputable charter operators will cancel or reschedule for free if conditions are unsafe. The captain has final authority on whether to depart. Light rain alone usually does not qualify for cancellation. Always check the operator's specific weather/cancellation policy before booking.
